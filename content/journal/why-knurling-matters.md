Knurling is the crosshatched pattern cut into the steel of a barbell or dumbbell handle. It turns a smooth surface into rows of tiny raised diamonds that bite into your hand and chalk. Nobody buys equipment for its knurling, but it decides whether a heavy set ends because your legs gave out or because the bar slipped.

## What knurling does

A smooth steel handle gets slippery the moment you sweat. Knurling gives your skin, and any chalk on it, something to grip. The more securely you can hold the bar, the less effort you spend squeezing it, and the more goes into the lift.

It's not only about heavy deadlifts. On presses and rows, a secure grip keeps your wrist stacked and the load where it should be. On dumbbells, it stops the handle from rotating in your hand at the top of a curl or a press.

## Not all knurl is the same

Knurling varies in three ways:

- **Depth**: how far the diamonds rise above the shaft. Deeper knurl grips harder and wears your hands faster.
- **Pitch**: how closely the diamonds are packed. A tight pitch feels finer and more even. A wide pitch feels sharper at each point.
- **Shape**: whether the tips are sharp or slightly flattened. Two bars with the same depth can feel completely different because of this alone.

Manufacturers rarely publish all three, so you'll mostly hear knurl described as **passive**, **medium** or **aggressive**. These words aren't standardised between brands. The only reliable test is to hold the bar, or to ask the supplier for a close-up photo of the handle and an honest description.

## Choosing the right grip

**More aggressive is not automatically better.** A very sharp knurl is great for a max deadlift and miserable for twenty high-rep cleans. Match it to the work:

- **Heavy, low-rep strength work** (deadlifts, heavy rows): medium to aggressive.
- **General training and higher reps**: medium. This is the right answer for most home gyms and studios.
- **Olympic lifting and high-volume classes**: medium or passive, often without a centre knurl, so the bar doesn't scrape your neck and shins.
- **Shared gyms**: medium. Members have different hands and habits, and an aggressive knurl generates complaints.

For dumbbells, a medium knurl on a steel handle is the standard. Rubber-lined or chrome handles with a light knurl are comfortable, but they get slippery on heavy rows. For a set that goes up to 25 kg, like our [Dumbbell Set + Rack](/equipment/dumbbell-set-rack-25), a proper knurl earns its keep.

## Looking after it

Knurling collects chalk, sweat and skin. Left alone, it clogs up and grips less. It can also rust, especially on bare steel in a humid garage.

- Brush the knurl with a stiff nylon brush after heavy sessions, or at least weekly in a shared gym.
- Wipe the bar down with a dry cloth and, for bare steel, a light coat of oil every few weeks.
- Store bars horizontally or vertically in a proper holder, not leaning against a wall.

Avoid wire brushes on coated finishes; they strip the protection and speed up rust.

## What to ask a supplier

- A description of the knurl (passive, medium, aggressive) and a close-up photo.
- Whether there's a centre knurl, and where the knurl marks are.
- The handle finish and care guidance for your climate.
