"Commercial-grade" is one of the most overused phrases in fitness equipment. There's no single standard behind it in everyday selling, and a product description alone can't tell you how a machine will hold up to fifty people a day. What it should mean is simple: built for many users, many hours a day, for years. Here's how to check.

## Start with the intended use

Ask the manufacturer directly what the equipment is designed for:

- **Home use**: one or two people, an hour or so a day.
- **Light commercial**: hotels, offices, residential societies. Several users, moderate hours.
- **Full commercial**: gyms and studios with constant traffic from morning to night.

A good manufacturer will state this in the product documentation and the warranty. If the warranty for commercial use is shorter than for home use, or doesn't mention it at all, that tells you what the equipment was really built for.

## Frames and steel

For racks and machines, the frame is most of the story:

- **Tube size and wall thickness.** Commercial racks commonly use 75 × 75 mm uprights with around 3 mm walls. Home racks often use 50–60 mm tube with thinner walls. Our [Power Rack PRX](/equipment/power-rack-prx) uses 75 × 75 mm uprights with a 3 mm wall.
- **Welds.** Look for continuous, even welds at joints, not spot welds or visible gaps.
- **Hardware.** Bolts, pins and J-hooks should be solid and fit without play.
- **Finish.** Powder coat should be even and thick enough to survive plates and bars knocking into it.

## Stacks, cables and pulleys

On selectorised machines like a functional trainer or lat pulldown:

- **Weight stacks** should run smoothly on guide rods without sticking or rattling.
- **Cables** should be aircraft-style, coated steel with proper end fittings, and rated well above the stack weight.
- **Pulleys** should turn freely and quietly. Cheap pulleys wear fast and turn every rep into a grind.
- **Shrouds** around the stack keep fingers out, which matters more in a shared gym.

Cables and pulleys are wear parts. Ask how easily replacements can be sourced, and how quickly.

## Cardio: look at the motor and the duty rating

For treadmills, the motor tells you most of what you need:

- **AC vs DC.** Commercial treadmills generally use AC motors built for long, continuous running. Many home treadmills use DC motors.
- **Continuous-duty rating.** Horsepower figures can be quoted as "peak" or "continuous". Only the continuous figure tells you what the motor sustains. Our [Commercial Treadmill C3](/equipment/treadmill-c3) is specified with a 3 HP AC continuous-duty motor.
- **Deck and belt.** A wider, longer belt and a thicker deck handle more users and more running speeds comfortably.

## Upholstery and touch points

Seats and pads get the most visible wear in a shared gym. Commercial upholstery uses thicker, tear-resistant material over dense foam. Grips and handles should be replaceable.

## Warranty and service tell the truth

The warranty is where a manufacturer puts its money where its mouth is. Look at:

- **Separate terms** for frame, parts, moving parts and upholstery.
- **Whether commercial use is covered**, and for how long.
- **Who services it** in your city, and how quickly spare parts arrive.

A long frame warranty with a short parts warranty is normal. A warranty that excludes commercial use on "commercial" equipment is a red flag.

## Ask for evidence

Before you commit to a commercial fit-out, get these in writing:

1. Intended use (home, light commercial, commercial).
2. Frame specification: tube size, wall thickness, finish.
3. Rated loads and cable specifications.
4. Motor type and continuous-duty rating for cardio.
5. Warranty terms per component, including commercial use.
6. Service and spare-parts arrangements for your location.
