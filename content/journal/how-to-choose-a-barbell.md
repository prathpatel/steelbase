The barbell is the piece of equipment you'll touch most. Racks, benches and plates matter, but the bar is what's in your hands on every rep. It's also where cheap equipment shows first: a bent shaft, sleeves that grind, knurling that either shreds your palms or does nothing at all.

Start with the training you do, not with the specification sheet.

## Start with how you train

- **General strength training** (squat, bench, deadlift, rows, overhead press): you want a stiff, general-purpose bar with a medium knurl. This is most home and studio lifters.
- **Olympic weightlifting** (snatch, clean and jerk): you want a bar with some whip and sleeves that spin freely, so the bar turns over quickly.
- **Mixed or group training**: you want something durable and forgiving, with a finish that survives chalk, sweat and being dropped from the rack a hundred times a day.

If you're unsure, a general-purpose bar is the safe choice. It will handle nearly everything a home gym or studio throws at it.

## The numbers that matter

**Weight and length.** The standard men's Olympic bar is 20 kg and about 2.2 m long. The women's bar is 15 kg, a little shorter and with a thinner shaft. If you're equipping a space used by many people, having both is worth it.

**Shaft diameter.** Men's bars are usually 28–29 mm, women's bars 25 mm. A thicker shaft (around 29 mm) feels solid in the squat and bench. A slightly thinner one is easier to grip for deadlifts and pulls. For most people, 28 mm is the comfortable middle.

**Sleeve diameter.** Olympic bars have 50 mm sleeves to take Olympic plates with a 50 mm hole. Cheaper "standard" bars with 25–30 mm sleeves are a different system entirely; their plates won't fit an Olympic bar. Check this before you buy plates.

**Sleeve rotation.** Sleeves ride on bushings or bearings. Bushings are simpler and very durable, which is ideal for strength training. Bearings spin faster and suit Olympic lifting. What you don't want is a sleeve that barely turns: that twisting force ends up in your wrists and elbows.

## Knurl and markings

Knurling is the textured grip pattern cut into the shaft. We've written about it in more detail in [Why knurling matters](/journal/why-knurling-matters). Two quick points:

- A **centre knurl** helps the bar stay put on your back in the squat. It can scratch your neck and shins in cleans and deadlifts, which is why weightlifting bars often leave it out.
- **Knurl marks** are smooth rings used to line up your grip. Weightlifting bars usually have them 91 cm apart and powerlifting bars 81 cm apart; many general bars have both.

## Load rating and finish

A load rating tells you how much weight the bar is designed to carry. You'll see figures quoted as "tensile strength" in PSI, or as a maximum load in kilograms. They aren't measured the same way across manufacturers, so treat them as a way to compare bars from the same supplier rather than a universal score. What matters is that the rating comes from the manufacturer's own documentation, not a marketing line.

Finish affects how the bar feels and how it ages. Bare steel feels best but rusts quickly in humid weather, which rules it out for most Indian garages and terraces unless you look after it. Chrome, zinc and black oxide trade a little grip feel for protection. In humid cities or unconditioned spaces, a protected finish plus a monthly wipe-down with a nylon brush will add years to the bar.

## Before you buy

Ask for these in writing:

1. Bar weight, length, shaft and sleeve diameter.
2. Knurl pattern and whether there's a centre knurl.
3. Sleeve rotation (bushings or bearings).
4. Load rating, and how it was measured.
5. Finish, and care guidance for your climate.
6. Warranty: what it covers (bending, sleeve failure) and for how long.
